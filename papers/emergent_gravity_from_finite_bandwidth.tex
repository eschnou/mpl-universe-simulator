\title{Emergent Gravity from Bandwidth Constraints in a Message-Passing Lattice}

\author{Laurent Eschenauer}
\email{laurent@eschenauer.be}
\affiliation{Independent Researcher}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We introduce message-passing lattices (MPLs) as a minimal computational substrate: nodes with bounded local state, connected by links of finite bandwidth, updated by a uniform local rule. Neighbor states are not instantly visible but must be transmitted via messages, and transmission time grows with message size.

This architecture poses a synchronization problem. Fully synchronous updates—where each node waits for all neighbors—allow a single slow region to freeze the entire network. Fully asynchronous updates—where nodes proceed independently—permit unbounded drift between regions. We propose bounded staleness as a middle path: each node adjusts its pace based on its own transmission delay and the average delay of its neighbors.

When local activity is high, state changes are large, messages are large, and links saturate. Saturated links cause delays. Delays spread through synchronization pressure. The result is a position-dependent reduction in update rate, which we describe by a scalar time-sag field $\phi(x) \leq 0$.

From the time-sag field, gravitational phenomenology follows: clocks in congested regions run slow (time dilation) and propagating patterns refract toward deeper $\phi$ (free fall). Numerical simulations on a 2D lattice confirm the predicted field profile, orbital motion, and flyby deflection.

The usual intuition treats gravity as a fundamental force that causes time dilation. Here the order is reversed: differential clock rates arise from bandwidth saturation, and gravitational attraction emerges as a consequence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Cellular automata (CA) are among the simplest models of distributed computation: a lattice of nodes, each updating its state based on the states of its neighbors, according to a uniform local rule. Despite this simplicity, CA exhibit remarkably rich behavior, from universal computation to self-organizing structures.

Standard CA rest on an implicit assumption: neighbor states are freely and instantly accessible. Each node reads its neighbors' current states, computes, and writes its new state, all in one atomic step. This idealization is convenient but unrealistic. In any physical or computational substrate, learning a neighbor's state requires communication, and communication has costs—in time, in energy, in bandwidth.

Message-passing lattices (MPLs) generalize CA by making these costs explicit. Nodes do not see neighbor states directly; they receive messages. Links have finite capacity, and transmission time grows with message size. This raises a question that standard CA never face: how should nodes synchronize their updates when communication is neither free nor instantaneous?

This paper explores the consequences of that question. We show that a natural synchronization policy—bounded staleness, where nodes adjust their pace to limit drift from neighbors—combined with finite bandwidth has consequences that extend well beyond the local neighborhood. High-activity regions saturate their links, causing delays that spread through synchronization pressure. The result is a smooth, position-dependent reduction in update rate across the lattice.

With full coupling—where nodes match the average pace of their neighbors—the emergent field satisfies a Poisson equation sourced by activity density, with solutions matching the Newtonian gravitational potential. Clocks in congested regions run slow, and propagating patterns deflect toward high-activity regions. We do not claim this is gravity—only that bandwidth constraints on a discrete substrate produce dynamics with the same mathematical form.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:mpl} defines the message-passing lattice substrate. Section~\ref{sec:sync} introduces the synchronization problem and bounded staleness. Section~\ref{sec:timesag} defines the time-sag field and sketches its derivation. Section~\ref{sec:phenomenology} develops the gravitational analogy. Section~\ref{sec:simulator} presents numerical validation. Section~\ref{sec:extensions} discusses extensions, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Message-Passing Lattices}
\label{sec:mpl}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Architecture}
\label{subsec:architecture}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A message-passing lattice (MPL) is a discrete computational substrate with one defining property: \emph{neighbor states are not instantly visible}. Each node knows only its own state; to learn about neighbors, it must receive explicit messages. The basic structure is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpl}.

Formally, an MPL consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A graph $G = (V, E)$ with vertices (nodes) $x \in V$ and edges (links) $e \in E$.
    \item A local state register $s_x$ at each node $x$, with bounded size.
    \item Bidirectional links, each with finite capacity $C$ bits per update opportunity.
    \item A uniform local update rule applied at every node.
\end{itemize}

Each node has bounded degree: $\deg(x) \leq d_{\max}$ for some fixed constant. This enforces locality. The graph need not be regular; irregularities can encode structure. The graph topology defines the effective spatial structure; we do not assume any background space.

A cellular automaton is a special case of an MPL where nodes transmit their full state to all neighbors on every cycle. The MPL framework is more general: nodes may transmit partial information, compressed representations, or state deltas, and the cost of transmission becomes an explicit part of the dynamics.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\columnwidth]{figures/mpl.png}
    \caption{A message-passing lattice. Each node $x$ has local state $s_x$ and updates based on incoming messages from neighbors. Neighbor states are not directly visible; information must be explicitly transmitted.}
    \label{fig:mpl}
\end{figure}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Update Dynamics}
\label{subsec:dynamics}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The engine evolves through local update cycles. Each cycle at node $x$ consists of two stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compute}: Read incoming messages and current state $s_x$. Apply the local transition rule to produce a new state $s'_x$ and outgoing messages.
    \item \textbf{Propagate}: Transmit a message $m_x$ encoding the state change $\Delta s_x = s'_x - s_x$ to all neighbors.
\end{enumerate}

The compute stage has fixed cost. The propagate stage does not: when the state change $\Delta s_x$ is large, the message $m_x$ is large, and transmission takes longer. This asymmetry between computation and communication is the source of all that follows.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Time from Updates}
\label{subsec:time}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is no global clock. Nodes update asynchronously, and the engine's history is a partial order of local update events, not a sequence of global states.

The intrinsic time recorded by a node is its \emph{proper time} $\tau_x$: the number of update cycles that node $x$ actually completes. A clock is any localized pattern that cycles through distinguishable states, advancing one step per completed update. Two clocks can compare their accumulated $\tau$ when they meet, but there is no substrate-level reference against which distant clocks are compared.

For analysis we introduce an external \emph{coordinate time} $t$ that counts \emph{update opportunities}—steps of a fair scheduler that selects nodes to attempt updates. This $t$ is not an observable of the MPL; it is a bookkeeping device used to define throughput constraints ($C$ bits per opportunity) and completion rates:
\begin{equation}
f(x) \;=\; \frac{d\tau_x}{dt} \;\in\; [0,1]
\label{eq:completion-rate}
\end{equation}
In a quiet region where every update opportunity completes, $f(x) = 1$. In a congested region where some cycles stall, $f(x) < 1$. The variation of $f(x)$ across the lattice is the origin of gravitational time dilation.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{The Synchronization Problem}
\label{sec:sync}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Finite Bandwidth}
\label{subsec:finite_bandwidth}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each link can carry at most $C$ bits per update opportunity. What happens when this capacity is insufficient?

Nodes transmit state deltas $\Delta s_x$ rather than full states. The size of the message $|m_x|$ scales with the magnitude of the state change. We define the \emph{activity} $a(x)$ at node $x$ as a measure of local dynamics—higher activity means larger state changes and larger messages. The transmission time for a message is:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{local\_delay}(x) \;=\; \frac{|m_x|}{C} \;\sim\; \frac{a(x)}{C}
    \label{eq:local-delay}
\end{equation}
When $|m_x| > C$, transmission cannot complete in one update opportunity and the node stalls.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Synchronous vs.\ Asynchronous Updates}
\label{subsec:sync_async}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

How should nodes coordinate their updates? Two extremes bracket the design space.

\textbf{Synchronous updates.} Each node waits for all neighbors' messages before proceeding. This ensures consistency: every node sees fresh neighbor data. But it couples the entire lattice. If one node stalls—because its message is too large for the available bandwidth—its neighbors must wait. Their neighbors must wait for them. A single congested region propagates outward until the entire network runs at the pace of its slowest node.

\textbf{Asynchronous updates.} Each node proceeds independently, using whatever neighbor information is available. This avoids global stalls: fast regions race ahead while slow regions lag behind. But without any coupling, the drift between regions grows without bound. Nodes that should be neighbors in any meaningful sense may find themselves arbitrarily far apart in update count. Locality breaks down.

Neither extreme is satisfactory. Synchronous updates produce global fragility; asynchronous updates destroy coherent structure. These two extremes are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:sync-async}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{figures/sync.png}
    \hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{figures/async.png}
    \caption{Two synchronization extremes. (a) Synchronous: node $s_1$ waits for all neighbors before updating; a single slow node stalls the lattice. (b) Asynchronous: nodes update independently; fast nodes race ahead, leading to unbounded drift.}
    \label{fig:sync-async}
\end{figure}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Bounded Staleness}
\label{subsec:bounded_staleness}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The solution is \emph{bounded staleness}: nodes adjust their pace to limit drift from neighbors, without requiring lockstep synchronization. This is not a new idea. Analogous mechanisms appear throughout distributed systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Backpressure} in network protocols, where producers slow down when consumers cannot keep up.
    \item \emph{Bounded staleness consistency} in distributed databases, which caps drift between replicas without enforcing synchronous commits.
    \item \emph{Gradient clock synchronization}, which minimizes clock skew between nearby nodes in a network.
\end{itemize}
The common thread is local feedback: each node adjusts its pace based on information from immediate neighbors, and global coordination emerges without central control.

Many rules could implement bounded staleness. We adopt a simple one. Let $\langle\texttt{delay}\rangle_x$ be the average delay among neighbors of node $x$, inferred from gaps between received messages. The node schedules its next update as:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{next\_update}(x) \;=\; \max\bigl(\texttt{local\_delay}(x),\; \alpha \cdot \langle\texttt{delay}\rangle_x\bigr)
    \label{eq:sync-rule}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls coupling strength.

This rule interpolates between extremes of coupling strength:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha = 0$: No coupling. Nodes proceed based on local delay alone—effectively asynchronous.
    \item $\alpha = 1$: Full coupling. A node slows to match the mean pace of its neighborhood.
\end{itemize}

Note that even $\alpha = 1$ does not recover the strict synchronous policy of Section~\ref{subsec:sync_async}, which would require waiting for the slowest neighbor rather than matching the average. Full coupling to the average is a softer constraint that still permits some drift while ensuring that congestion propagates through the lattice.

Intermediate values $0 < \alpha < 1$ damp the coupling: nodes feel their neighbors but the influence is attenuated. As we discuss in the next section, the choice of $\alpha$ has consequences for the range of the emergent interaction.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{The Time-Sag Field}
\label{sec:timesag}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Definition}
\label{subsec:timesag_def}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In steady state, each node settles into a characteristic completion rate $f(x) \in [0,1]$: the fraction of update opportunities that result in completed cycles. We define the \emph{time-sag field} as the deviation from the uncongested rate:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) \;\equiv\; f(x) - 1 \;\leq\; 0
    \label{eq:timesag-def}
\end{equation}

The interpretation is direct:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\phi(x) = 0$: quiet region, all updates complete.
    \item $\phi(x) < 0$: congested region, some updates stall.
\end{itemize}

Active regions sit in potential wells, with $\phi$ becoming more negative where congestion is worse. The field satisfies $\phi(x) \in [-1, 0]$, with $\phi = -1$ representing complete congestion.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Field Behavior}
\label{subsec:field_behavior}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The synchronization rule couples neighboring nodes: when a node's neighbors are slow, it slows down to maintain bounded staleness. This coupling causes local congestion to spread through the lattice, producing a smooth field rather than isolated pockets of slowdown.

The strength of coupling matters. If nodes couple weakly to their neighbors, congestion dissipates quickly with distance. If nodes couple fully—matching the average pace of their neighborhood—the influence propagates without attenuation and the resulting field is long-ranged.

To see why, consider steady state with full coupling ($\alpha = 1$). The synchronization rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:sync-rule}) involves a $\max$ of local and neighbor delays. In steady state, when both contributions are comparable, this relaxes to a sum: each node's delay $\delta(x)$ matches the average delay of its neighbors, plus a local contribution from activity:
\begin{equation}
    \delta(x) \;=\; \langle\delta\rangle_x \;+\; \gamma\, a(x)
    \label{eq:heuristic}
\end{equation}
Rearranging, $\delta(x) - \langle\delta\rangle_x = \gamma\, a(x)$. The left-hand side is proportional to the discrete Laplacian $(L\delta)(x) = d[\delta(x) - \langle\delta\rangle_x]$. In the continuum limit, this becomes $\nabla^2\phi = \kappa\,\rho_{\mathrm{act}}$, Poisson's equation with activity density as source. Details appear in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}.

We show in Section~\ref{sec:simulator} that with full coupling, the emergent $\phi(x)$ field around a localized activity source matches the solution to Poisson's equation, with activity density playing the role of mass density. In two dimensions this gives $\phi \propto -\log(r/r_0)$; in three dimensions it would give $\phi \propto -1/r$.

\section{Gravitational Phenomenology}
\label{sec:phenomenology}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Time Dilation}
\label{subsec:time_dilation}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A clock is a localized pattern whose internal state cycles through distinguishable phases, advancing one step per completed update. The accumulated step count is the proper time $\tau_x$ at node $x$.

In a quiet region where $f(x) = 1$, the clock advances once per update opportunity. In a congested region where $f(x) < 1$, some update opportunities fail to complete and the clock advances more slowly. Since $\phi(x) = f(x) - 1$, the proper time evolves as:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\tau}{dt} = 1 + \phi(x)
    \label{eq:time-dilation}
\end{equation}
Clocks in congested regions ($\phi < 0$) run slow relative to clocks in quiet regions ($\phi = 0$). This is gravitational time dilation: clocks near massive bodies—here, regions of high activity—tick at a reduced rate. The effect arises not from any explicit time-warping mechanism but from the local failure of update cycles to complete.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Free Fall}
\label{subsec:free_fall}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Patterns propagate through the lattice by successive state updates along connected nodes. In a region of uniform $\phi$, wavefronts advance symmetrically and trajectories are straight. When $\phi$ varies, the portion of a wavefront in deeper $\phi$ (more congested) completes fewer updates per unit coordinate time, falls behind, and the trajectory bends toward the slower region (Fig.~\ref{fig:refraction}).

This is analogous to Fermat's principle: light refracts toward regions of lower propagation speed. Here, patterns refract toward regions of lower completion rate—toward deeper $\phi$. The deflection depends only on the local gradient of $\phi$ and the pattern's velocity, not on its internal structure or amplitude. A small perturbation and a large wavepacket follow the same trajectory through a given field.

    \caption{Refraction of a propagating pattern in the time-sag field. The portion of the wavefront nearer the source (deeper $\phi$) completes fewer updates and falls behind, tilting the wavefront and bending the trajectory inward.}

\section{Numerical Validation}
\label{sec:simulator}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

To test these predictions, we developed an open-source simulator implementing bandwidth-limited message-passing dynamics.\footnote{Available at \url{https://github.com/eschnou/mpl-universe-simulator}}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Simulation Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The simulator models a 2D square lattice with absorbing boundary conditions (boundary nodes update at the uncongested rate without delays). Each node $x$ carries local state, tracks completed update cycles, and implements the synchronization rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sync-rule} with $\alpha = 1$. Links have uniform capacity $C$, and activity $a(x)$ is specified as a static spatial distribution.

A localized mass is modeled as a circular region of elevated activity surrounded by a quiet background ($a = 0$). The simulation runs until the completion rate field $f(x)$ converges to steady state, from which we compute $\phi(x) = f(x) - 1$.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Radial Profile}
\label{subsec:radial_profile}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For a single localized source, the steady-state time-sag field is approximately radially symmetric. Figure~\ref{fig:field} shows the azimuthally averaged $f(r)$ as a function of distance from the source center.

In two dimensions, the Poisson equation yields a logarithmic potential: $\phi \propto -\log(r/r_0)$ outside the source. The simulated profile matches this prediction, confirming that the synchronization dynamics produce the expected long-range field. Deviations near the source boundary reflect the finite size of the active region and discretization effects.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{figures/radial_profile.png}
    \hfill

\caption{Steady-state time-sag field for a single localized source on a 2D lattice. %------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Orbital Motion}
\label{subsec:orbital}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To test free fall, we inject a propagating particle into the converged $\phi$ field and track its trajectory. The particle is initialized with a velocity perpendicular to the radial direction from the source. The resulting trajectories orbit the active region, consistent with motion in an attractive central potential (Fig.~\ref{fig:orbital}). In a 2D logarithmic potential, orbits generically precess rather than close; our simulations exhibit this expected behavior.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{figures/orbital.png}
      \caption{Orbital motion of a propagating particle around a localized activity source. In a 2D logarithmic potential, orbits precess rather than close.}
    \label{fig:orbital}
\end{figure}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Flyby Deflection}
\label{subsec:flyby}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A more direct test of the effective force law is gravitational deflection. We initialize a particle on a trajectory that would, in the absence of the source, pass at some closest-approach distance $r_{\min}$. We then measure the asymptotic deflection angle between incoming and outgoing directions. Figure~\ref{fig:flyby} shows a flyby with deflection angles consistent with scattering in a logarithmic potential.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{figures/flyby.png}
    \caption{Gravitational flyby deflection. A particle passing the source at closest approach $r_{\min} \approx 13$ lattice units is deflected by approximately $34°$. Dashed line shows the undeflected trajectory.}
    \label{fig:flyby}
\end{figure}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The current implementation is two-dimensional, so potentials are logarithmic rather than $1/r$. Extension to three dimensions is straightforward in principle but computationally demanding.

The activity distribution is static and externally imposed. The simulations therefore validate that imposed activity produces the predicted field and dynamics, but do not yet demonstrate self-consistent sourcing by propagating patterns. Whether a wavepacket can generate sufficient activity to measurably influence other patterns remains to be tested.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Toward General Relativity}
\label{sec:extensions}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The time-sag field $\phi(x)$ is a scalar. This suffices for Newtonian gravity but not for general relativity, which requires a rank-2 metric tensor.

Our model tracks only total activity at each node - a single number. But if the update rule consults a multi-hop neighborhood, the engine naturally sees not just how much activity there is, but how it is distributed spatially and whether information flows asymmetrically.

This suggests a route to richer structure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Total activity} sources the time-sag field (gravitational time dilation).
    \item \emph{Directional flow}—asymmetric bandwidth usage in different directions—might source frame-dragging effects.
    \item \emph{Anisotropic congestion} could provide the spatial metric components.
\end{itemize}

We have not derived the Einstein equations, but some of the architectural ingredients are present. Developing this into a full tensorial theory is left for future work.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We have shown that message-passing lattices—a generalization of cellular automata with explicit communication costs—can produce long-range collective behavior from purely local rules.

The core mechanism is a synchronization problem. In a message-passing lattice, nodes must coordinate their updates despite having no global clock. Fully synchronous updates cause global fragility; fully asynchronous updates allow unbounded drift. Bounded staleness—where nodes adjust their pace to limit drift from neighbors—provides a middle path. When combined with finite link capacity, this synchronization pressure transforms local congestion into a smooth, lattice-wide slowdown field.

With full coupling ($\alpha = 1$), the resulting time-sag field $\phi(x)$ satisfies a Poisson equation sourced by activity density, yielding $1/r$ potentials in three dimensions and logarithmic potentials in two. Clocks in congested regions tick slowly, and propagating patterns refract toward regions of deeper $\phi$. These dynamics mirror gravitational time dilation and free fall, arising here from bandwidth constraints rather than spacetime curvature.

Numerical simulations on a 2D lattice validate these findings. The steady-state $\phi$ field around a localized activity source shows the expected logarithmic profile. Propagating particles exhibit orbital motion and flyby deflection consistent with an attractive central potential.

Several questions remain open. The current simulations impose activity externally; whether propagating patterns can self-consistently source the fields they traverse—so that two wavepackets attract each other through the congestion they each generate—has not been demonstrated. Extension to three dimensions would test whether the mechanism scales correctly. And the relationship between coupling strength and field behavior deserves further study.

Nonetheless, the central result stands: a discrete network with bounded communication, operating under a simple local synchronization rule, produces a long-range attractive interaction with the mathematical structure of Newtonian gravity. The usual intuition treats gravity as a force that causes time dilation; here the order is reversed. Differential clock rates arise first, from bandwidth saturation, and gravitational attraction follows as a consequence. This suggests that gravity-like behavior may be a natural consequence of resource constraints on any local, finite substrate.

\appendix
\section{Derivation of the Field Equation}
\label{app:derivation}

In steady state with full coupling ($\alpha = 1$), each node's delay satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \delta(x) = \max\bigl(\gamma a(x) + 1,\; \langle\delta\rangle_x\bigr)
\end{equation}
In the weak-congestion regime where both terms contribute comparably, self-consistency requires:
\begin{equation}
    \delta(x) - \langle\delta\rangle_x \approx \gamma\, a(x)
\end{equation}
On a regular lattice with degree $d$, the graph Laplacian satisfies $(L\delta)(x) = d[\delta(x) - \langle\delta\rangle_x]$, giving:
\begin{equation}
    (L\delta)(x) = d\gamma\, a(x)
\end{equation}
In the continuum limit, $L \to -\ell^2\nabla^2$. Defining $\phi = 1 - \delta$ (so $\phi \leq 0$ in congested regions), we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2\phi = \kappa\,\rho_{\mathrm{act}}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa > 0$. This is Poisson's equation, yielding $\phi \propto -1/r$ in 3D and $\phi \propto \ln(r/R)$ in 2D.

For $\alpha < 1$, an additional $(1-\alpha)\phi$ term appears, producing a screened Poisson equation with finite-range solutions.
